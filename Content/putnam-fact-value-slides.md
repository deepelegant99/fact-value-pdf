## Slide 1 — What makes a society successful?

An economy grows. A government survives. An empire expands.

**What would make these achievements worth celebrating?**

Hilary Putnam and *The Entanglement of Fact and Value*

---

## Slide 2 — Social science explains how societies work

- Economics investigates production, wages, investment, and employment.
- Political science investigates power, cooperation, conflict, and institutional survival.
- History investigates how people and institutions changed.

**We also want to understand whether those changes improved human life.**

---

## Slide 3 — An economy grows. Are people better off?

Imagine a country where:

- Economic output grows by **5%**.
- Median purchasing power remains unchanged.
- Working hours increase.
- Preventable illness becomes more common.

**Has this country made progress?**

*Hypothetical example.*

---

## Slide 4 — “Progress” requires a standard

Different standards bring different features into view:

- **Production:** How much does the economy produce?
- **Living standards:** What can ordinary households afford?
- **Well-being:** How healthy, secure, and fulfilled are people?
- **Freedom:** What meaningful choices can people make?

Agreeing on the measurements does not settle which standard best captures progress.

---

## Slide 5 — A useful distinction can become a sweeping claim

We can distinguish:

- **Descriptive claims:** “Average working hours increased.”
- **Evaluative claims:** “These working conditions are unacceptable.”

A stronger position adds:

**Descriptions can be objectively justified; evaluations ultimately express preferences.**

Putnam challenges this stronger position. Merely distinguishing description from evaluation does not establish it. [Putnam’s book](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctv1pdrpz4)

---

## Slide 6 — Equal income can conceal unequal opportunities

Imagine two people with the same income:

- One can travel and work without additional assistance.
- The other needs expensive mobility support and faces inaccessible transport.
- Their purchasing power is equal, but their practical opportunities differ.

**What would an adequate comparison of their circumstances measure?**

*Illustration of Sen’s argument in [“Equality of What?”](https://tannerlectures.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/sen80.pdf).*

---

## Slide 7 — A better question can reveal overlooked facts

Amartya Sen proposes examining people’s **capabilities**: their actual opportunities to do and be things they have reason to value.

This directs attention to:

- Mobility and accessibility.
- Nutrition and shelter.
- Participation in community life.

A judgment about what matters changes what researchers investigate. [Sen’s lecture](https://tannerlectures.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/sen80.pdf)

---

## Slide 8 — Satisfaction can be an incomplete measure

People can adapt to difficult circumstances and lower their expectations.

- Reported satisfaction tells us something important.
- It can coexist with restricted opportunities.
- Deciding whether it adequately measures well-being requires further judgment.

**Does becoming accustomed to deprivation make deprivation disappear?**

[Sen’s discussion of utility and capability](https://tannerlectures.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/sen80.pdf)

---

## Slide 9 — A deeply unequal allocation can be efficient

A simplified economy contains two people and 100 units of one good.

Assume:

- The supply is fixed.
- Each person prefers having more.
- Neither cares about the other’s allocation.

One receives **99 units**; the other receives **1**.

Helping either person requires taking something from the other.

---

## Slide 10 — Efficiency answers a limited question

The 99–1 allocation is **Pareto efficient**: no feasible change helps someone without hurting someone else.

Under the same assumptions, **50–50 is also Pareto efficient**.

The criterion does not tell us which distribution is fair.

**Mathematical precision does not make a criterion morally sufficient.**

---

## Slide 11 — Can we criticize our measures of success?

Suppose a welfare measure systematically overlooks serious, avoidable suffering.

Can we give reasons for changing it?

- It may omit conditions central to people’s lives.
- It may treat relevantly different circumstances as equivalent.
- It may reward outcomes that undermine its stated purpose.

**Are these criticisms rationally assessable—or simply competing preferences?**

---

## Slide 12 — A government can survive through different means

A political model might explain stability through:

- Reliable taxation.
- Effective administration.
- Cooperation among powerful groups.
- Public support—or effective intimidation.

**Explaining why a government survives leaves open whether its survival is desirable.**

---

## Slide 13 — Public order needs interpretation

Imagine that demonstrations sharply decline.

Possible explanations include:

- Citizens are more satisfied.
- Grievances have been addressed.
- Organizing has become dangerous.
- People believe protest is futile.

The same headline statistic can accompany very different political conditions.

**We need to investigate what the silence means.**

---

## Slide 14 — Power, acceptance, and justification differ

| Claim | Question it answers |
|---|---|
| The government secures obedience. | How effective is its power? |
| Citizens believe it has a right to rule. | What do citizens believe? |
| It has a right to rule. | Is its authority justified? |

Evidence for one claim does not automatically establish the others.

---

## Slide 15 — Institutions embody choices that remain open to criticism

Property rules, voting procedures, and tax systems organize whose claims receive recognition.

We can ask:

- How did these arrangements emerge?
- What consequences do they produce?
- What reasons support keeping or changing them?

**The fact that earlier values shaped an institution does not establish that its present rules are justified.**

---

## Slide 16 — Historical questions shape what becomes visible

Consider a history of an empire.

A focus on administration directs attention to taxes, roads, and military organization.

A focus on ordinary life directs attention to labor, displacement, security, and household survival.

**The questions we consider important shape the evidence we seek.**

Historians’ perspectives influence inquiry while remaining answerable to the record. [AHA standards](https://www.historians.org/resource/statement-on-standards-of-professional-conduct/)

---

## Slide 17 — Frederick Douglass challenged an interpretation

In his 1845 autobiography, Douglass described people mistaking enslaved people’s singing for evidence of contentment.

He explained that, in his experience, singing often expressed or relieved sorrow.

**An apparently familiar behavior had been misunderstood.**

Listening to the people being described can correct an observer’s interpretation. [Douglass, Chapter II](https://teachingamericanhistory.org/document/narrative-of-the-life-of-frederick-douglass-an-american-slave-chapter-ii/)

---

## Slide 18 — Historical judgment requires discipline

Taking people’s experiences seriously requires careful evidence.

- Read sources in their historical context.
- Investigate disagreements among people at the time.
- Examine evidence that challenges your interpretation.
- Separate explaining an action from defending it.

**A moral commitment does not excuse a distorted account of the past.**

[AHA standards for historical scholarship](https://www.historians.org/resource/statement-on-standards-of-professional-conduct/)

---

## Slide 19 — Our judgments about the past shape future goals

Imagine evaluating a past government.

- If success means survival, we may emphasize preserving authority.
- If success includes public security, we investigate whose security improved.
- If success includes freedom, we investigate people’s ability to challenge power.

**Historical lessons depend partly on what we judge worth repeating.**

Evidence can also force us to reconsider those judgments.

---

## Slide 20 — Interaction alone does not prove entanglement

So far, we have seen that:

- Values influence research questions and institutions.
- Evidence can change evaluations.
- Measures of success need justification.

A defender of the fact/value divide can accept all three.

**Putnam’s stronger challenge concerns whether description and evaluation can always be cleanly separated.**

---

## Slide 21 — Some concepts describe and evaluate together

Consider:

- Cruel.
- Honest.
- Courageous.

Philosophers call these **thick ethical concepts**.

They characterize conduct while also carrying evaluative significance.

**Can we fully explain what such a concept means by separating neutral description from approval or disapproval?**

This is a central question in Putnam’s argument. [The reading](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctv1pdrpz4)

---

## Slide 22 — What makes an action cruel?

Imagine a teacher repeatedly ridiculing a struggling student.

Assessing cruelty involves questions such as:

- What suffering or humiliation occurred?
- What did the teacher understand?
- How vulnerable was the student?
- What reasons, if any, justified the conduct?

**Pain alone cannot settle the judgment: necessary medical treatment can also hurt.**

---

## Slide 23 — Putnam challenges the proposed separation

A critic proposes:

“First describe the behavior neutrally. Then add disapproval.”

Putnam’s challenge:

- Can the neutral description identify everything relevant to cruelty?
- Do terms such as “gratuitous” or “humiliating” reintroduce evaluation?
- Can the separation preserve the concept across different cases?

**Whether this separation succeeds is a substantive dispute.** [Argument and criticism](https://www.nordprag.org/papers/Niiniluoto%20-%20Facts%20and%20Values.pdf)

---

## Slide 24 — Scientific inquiry also depends on standards

Scientists assess explanations using:

- Fit with evidence.
- Coherence.
- Explanatory power.
- Simplicity, where appropriate.

These are **epistemic values**: standards for good inquiry.

Putnam emphasizes that judging a theory worthy of acceptance involves standards about how we ought to reason. [Putnam, “Beyond the Fact/Value Dichotomy”](https://inters.org/Putnam-Fact-Value)

---

## Slide 25 — Disagreement does not automatically defeat rational judgment

Researchers can disagree about:

- Which explanation is more adequate.
- Whether an assumption is reasonable.
- How to weigh competing theoretical virtues.

There may be no mechanical procedure that settles every case.

Putnam asks why similar difficulties should automatically disqualify ethical judgment from rational assessment. [Putnam’s argument](https://inters.org/Putnam-Fact-Value)

---

## Slide 26 — The strongest objection distinguishes kinds of value

A scientifically minded critic can reply:

“Standards for discovering truth differ from standards for choosing how to live.”

That objection deserves an answer.

- Science needing standards does not automatically establish moral objectivity.
- Putnam must explain why ethical judgments can also be justified.

**The argument puts pressure on the divide; further reasoning is still required.**

---

## Slide 27 — Entanglement does not settle every moral question

Putnam’s challenge does not establish that:

- Every factual statement is a moral judgment.
- Every sincere evaluation is correct.
- Economic growth automatically creates an obligation.
- One political system follows uniquely from the evidence.

A premise such as “this policy is cruel” already contains evaluation.

**Using that premise does not derive morality from wholly neutral premises.**

---

## Slide 28 — Evaluative standards can themselves face criticism

Suppose we call a policy fair because everyone receives the same payment.

We can examine that standard:

- Does equal payment address substantially different needs?
- Does the rule serve its stated purpose?
- Can its justification survive relevant counterexamples?
- What evidence would make us revise it?

**Evaluation can involve argument, investigation, and correction.**

---

## Slide 29 — The larger issue is the reach of reason

The stakes extend across disciplines:

- **Economics:** Can we justify what counts as welfare?
- **Politics:** Can we assess authority as well as explain power?
- **History:** Can we defend judgments about progress and failure?
- **Science:** How do standards of good inquiry earn their authority?

**Can reason help assess our goals as well as our methods?**

---

## Slide 30 — Why read Putnam?

*“The Entanglement of Fact and Value”*  
Hilary Putnam, 2002, Chapter 2, pages 28–45.

Read it to test three questions:

- Can understanding a human situation require evaluative concepts?
- Can value judgments be rationally justified?
- What would objectivity require if inquiry includes values?

**Understanding how a society works raises the further question of what makes it worth sustaining.**
