# OBS Narration Notes

Deck: **Can Facts Really Be Separated from Values?**

Suggested total runtime: **12–15 minutes**

Use these as speaking cues rather than a script. Pause briefly after each opening claim, and let the diagrams stay on screen long enough for viewers to follow the arrows and labels.

## Slide 1 — Can facts really be separated from values?

**Main claim:** The familiar separation between facts and values looks obvious, but Hilary Putnam argues that it is too clean.

**Explanation:** We usually treat a statement such as “the temperature is 90 degrees” as a fact and “cruelty is bad” as a value judgment. That distinction is useful. The question is whether it creates a hard boundary between two completely independent kinds of claim.

**Example:** Point to the two boxes and say, “These examples look as if they belong in separate worlds.”

**Transition:** “Let’s begin with the traditional picture Putnam wants us to examine.”

## Slide 2 — The traditional dichotomy

**Main claim:** The traditional dichotomy treats facts and values as separate domains.

**Explanation:** Facts appear to cover observation, measurement, empirical claims, and theory. Values appear to cover good and bad, right and wrong, rational and irrational, and what we ought to do. The formula in the center says that the two sets do not overlap.

**Example:** A thermometer reading seems factual, while condemning an action seems evaluative.

**Transition:** “But even before we reach Putnam’s main argument, this wall begins to leak.”

## Slide 3 — The first crack: mutual influence

**Main claim:** Facts and values already influence one another in ordinary inquiry and social life.

**Explanation:** Our priorities affect which laws we write, which research we fund, and which technologies we design. Once those choices produce consequences, the new facts can change what we value. So the relationship runs in both directions.

**Example:** If a city values safety, it may fund a transport policy. Evidence about who benefits or gets excluded may then change the city’s idea of fairness.

**Transition:** “That interaction matters, but it still does not prove Putnam’s stronger claim.”

## Slide 4 — Interaction is not yet entanglement

**Main claim:** Two things can affect each other while remaining conceptually distinct.

**Explanation:** This is an important objection. A thermostat controls a furnace, and the furnace changes what the thermostat detects. They interact continuously, but we can still identify them as separate things. Mutual influence alone therefore does not establish fact–value entanglement.

**Example:** Trace the arrows between the thermostat and furnace, then emphasize the not-equal sign at the bottom.

**Transition:** “Putnam needs more than interaction, and his strongest example comes from what philosophers call thick ethical concepts.”

## Slide 5 — Enter the thick concept

**Main claim:** A thick ethical concept describes conduct and evaluates it at the same time.

**Explanation:** Consider the word “cruel.” Calling the guard cruel tells us something about how the guard acted, but it also condemns that conduct. The descriptive and evaluative aspects seem to travel together inside the concept.

**Example:** “The guard behaved cruelly toward the prisoner” does more than report a physical movement. It interprets the treatment as wrongful suffering.

**Transition:** “A defender of the clean divide can still try to separate those two parts.”

## Slide 6 — The tempting decomposition

**Main claim:** The obvious rescue attempt defines cruelty as neutral facts plus a separate negative evaluation.

**Explanation:** On this view, we first list what happened: the guard hit the prisoner, withheld food, or caused pain. Then we add the judgment that this conduct was bad or wrong. If that works, the traditional boundary survives.

**Example:** Point from “cruel” to the two boxes and ask, “Can we really reconstruct the full concept by simply adding D and E?”

**Transition:** “Putnam’s answer is: not so fast, because the neutral list does not interpret itself.”

## Slide 7 — Putnam: not so fast

**Main claim:** Evaluative understanding helps determine which facts are relevant and what they mean.

**Explanation:** Causing pain might count as cruelty, but it might also occur during necessary medical treatment. Following policy might excuse an action in one setting and reveal indifference in another. To recognize humiliation for amusement as relevant, we already need some understanding of cruelty.

**Example:** Contrast “caused pain” with “humiliated for amusement.” The physical outcome alone does not settle the judgment.

**Transition:** “We now need a model that preserves the difference between fact and value without pretending that they never overlap.”

## Slide 8 — A visual model of judgment

**Main claim:** We can picture a judgment as having both descriptive and evaluative content.

**Explanation:** This coordinate system is only a teaching device; it is not Putnam’s mathematics. The horizontal axis represents descriptive content, and the vertical axis represents evaluative content. A judgment can contain different amounts of each without making facts and values identical.

**Example:** The point in the middle represents a judgment that tells us something about the world while also assessing it.

**Transition:** “With this map in place, we can look first at judgments near the descriptive end.”

## Slide 9 — Some judgments lean descriptive

**Main claim:** Some judgments are overwhelmingly descriptive, even if inquiry is never completely detached from standards.

**Explanation:** “The thermometer reads 22 degrees Celsius” mainly reports an observation. That is why the point sits close to the descriptive axis. Still, choices about instruments, relevance, accuracy, and acceptable evidence depend on standards of good inquiry.

**Example:** We can check the reading against another calibrated thermometer. That test relies on a standard for what counts as reliable measurement.

**Transition:** “At the other end, some judgments carry much more evaluative weight.”

## Slide 10 — Some judgments lean evaluative

**Main claim:** Highly evaluative judgments still contain some descriptive understanding.

**Explanation:** Statements such as “that was admirable” or “that was disgusting” clearly express evaluation. Yet they are not empty reactions. To apply them appropriately, we need some account of what happened and why it deserves that response.

**Example:** Calling an act admirable normally implies that someone faced a difficulty or risk and responded in a way we respect.

**Transition:** “Between these two extremes lies the region most important to Putnam’s argument.”

## Slide 11 — The thick region

**Main claim:** Many concepts central to human life combine description and evaluation.

**Explanation:** Words such as cruel, brave, unjust, dishonest, and reasonable do not sit neatly on one axis. They identify features of conduct while also positioning that conduct within a framework of judgment. Their meaning would be diminished if we removed either side.

**Example:** “Dishonest” describes more than speaking falsely; context, intention, and an expectation of truthfulness all matter.

**Transition:** “So far the picture is static. The next step is to see how judgments change over time.”

## Slide 12 — Static entanglement is only the beginning

**Main claim:** Both the factual and evaluative dimensions of judgment can change over time.

**Explanation:** At one moment, we judge a situation using the facts and standards available to us. New evidence can alter the factual picture, while reflection or experience can alter the standards. The later judgment can therefore occupy a different point on both axes.

**Example:** A policy may initially seem fair, then evidence about unequal effects may lead us to revise both our description of the policy and our understanding of fairness.

**Transition:** “Let’s separate that motion into two directions, beginning with the way values guide inquiry.”

## Slide 13 — Values guide inquiry

**Main claim:** Inquiry begins with standards, interests, and priorities that influence the questions we ask.

**Explanation:** Values do not dictate the answer, but they help determine what counts as important, what evidence we seek, and what makes an explanation successful. Even scientific inquiry uses standards such as simplicity, evidential support, and scope. In social inquiry, fairness may also affect what we decide to examine.

**Example:** A study of a public program changes when researchers ask only about total cost versus also asking who can actually access it.

**Transition:** “The influence also runs back the other way: inquiry can reshape the standards with which it began.”

## Slide 14 — Inquiry reshapes value

**Main claim:** Evidence and successful inquiry can revise our evaluative standards.

**Explanation:** We begin with a set of values, build a theory or practice around them, and then observe the results. Those results may expose a blind spot or reveal that one standard matters more than we thought. The revised values then guide the next round of inquiry.

**Example:** A fairness rule based on equal treatment may change after evidence shows that people face substantially different barriers.

**Transition:** “This feedback loop is easier to see when values become embedded in objects we use every day.”

## Slide 15 — Artifact loop

**Main claim:** Values shape designed objects, and using those objects can reshape future values and expectations.

**Explanation:** Designers build ideas about convenience, beauty, privacy, or accessibility into an interface. Once people live with that design, the experience can normalize new expectations. The artifact becomes part of the environment in which later judgments form.

**Example:** A phone interface that makes accessibility features standard can change what users expect every digital product to provide.

**Transition:** “The same pattern applies more broadly to any person acting in the world.”

## Slide 16 — World, belief, value, action

**Main claim:** We do not merely observe the world; our values help select actions that create the next world we encounter.

**Explanation:** The current world supplies evidence, evidence shapes belief, and belief interacts with what we value. Those values influence action. Action then changes the world, producing new evidence and a new round of evaluation.

**Example:** If I value accessibility, I may redesign a service. The results then show me which barriers I understood correctly and which values need refinement.

**Transition:** “We can summarize this repeated process as a coupled dynamical system.”

## Slide 17 — A coupled dynamical system

**Main claim:** Facts and values remain distinct, but their development is coupled.

**Explanation:** The factual state and the value state both help produce what comes next. New facts can revise values, while values influence which actions, institutions, and inquiries generate future facts. “Coupled” captures the middle position: the two are neither identical nor independent.

**Example:** Public health evidence can change what a society regards as a fair risk, while that idea of fairness shapes which health data it collects next.

**Transition:** “This brings us to Putnam’s deepest challenge to the idea of a completely value-free standpoint.”

## Slide 18 — Rationality is already normative

**Main claim:** Rational inquiry itself depends on standards about how we ought to reason.

**Explanation:** Following evidence, avoiding contradiction, revising beliefs, and preferring better explanations are not mere descriptions of what brains happen to do. They are norms we use to distinguish good reasoning from bad reasoning. If we remove every norm, rationality becomes only a causal account of thought.

**Example:** Saying that someone should revise a belief after contrary evidence already invokes a standard, not just a behavioral prediction.

**Transition:** “We can now state the conclusion without collapsing facts into values.”

## Slide 19 — Conclusion

**Main claim:** Facts and values are distinguishable, but our best descriptions and evaluations often cannot be cleanly separated.

**Explanation:** Putnam’s point is not that facts and values are the same. It is that they are not sealed off from each other. Thick concepts join description with evaluation, and over time facts and values can reshape one another through inquiry and action.

**Example:** The word “cruel” depends on evidence about what happened, but deciding which details count as cruelty requires evaluative understanding.

**Closing:** “So the real choice is not between pure fact and arbitrary value. The harder question is how facts and values work together, and how both can remain open to rational criticism.”
